Dear Editors,

We are pleased to submit our manuscript, "Random matrix theory and
Dyson-Brownian-motion diagnostics for breast cancer coexpression
networks: a Monte-Carlo-validated framework applied to TCGA-BRCA," for
consideration as a Research Article in PLOS Computational Biology.

This paper unifies a static random-matrix-theory (RMT) correlation
threshold, an explicit Monte Carlo permutation null, and a
Dyson-Brownian-motion level-repulsion diagnostic into a single
statistical framework, and applies it, unmodified beyond the one
data-specific preprocessing step each requires, across three real,
independent data modalities from the same disease: bulk RNA-seq
(TCGA-BRCA, comparing triple-negative and luminal A breast cancer),
single-cell RNA-seq, and Hi-C chromatin contact data. To our knowledge
this is the first application of a Dyson-repulsion diagnostic to real
gene-expression data at any resolution, and the first time one RMT
pipeline has been run across bulk, single-cell, and 3D genomic data for
the same cancer type. We believe this breadth, together with a
mathematically proved result connecting the static and dynamic
diagnostics (a corollary quantifying exactly which eigenvalue pairs
repel least reliably, and why), gives the paper a methodological
contribution beyond any one of its individual applications.

We think PLOS Computational Biology is the right venue for this work
for three reasons. First, the paper's central discipline --
validating every positive finding against an explicit statistical null,
and reporting negative results (a wavelet method that fails identically
under three unrelated ordering axes, a classification benchmark too
easy to discriminate feature-selection methods, and a training
diagnostic that sees only part of what it was built to see) with the
same rigor as positive ones -- matches the transparency and
reproducibility standard this journal is known for upholding. Second,
the paper sits precisely at the interface of applied random matrix
theory and computational cancer genomics that defines this journal's
scope. Third, every number in the paper is produced by included,
re-runnable code against public data (TCGA, GEO accessions GSE176078
and GSE167150, and UCSC reference annotation), consistent with the
journal's code and data availability requirements.

One of us (E.H.L.) is a computational systems biology researcher whose
own group's prior work on breast cancer coexpression networks
(cited in the manuscript) motivated this collaboration; the other
(J.C.G.) developed the RMT/Dyson-repulsion framework and carried out
the computational analysis.

This manuscript has not been published previously and is not under
consideration elsewhere. The authors declare no competing interests. We
believe it will be of interest to PLOS Computational Biology's readers
working on gene network inference, random matrix theory applications in
biology, and multi-omic data integration.

Thank you for your consideration.
